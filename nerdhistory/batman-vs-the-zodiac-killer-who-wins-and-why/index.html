Batman vs the Zodiac Killer: Who Wins and Why

We replay the Zodiac's four confirmed attacks in Gotham to test whether a man with no powers can catch a killer four real investigations never named.

Batman catches the Zodiac in Gotham, but only because fiction supplies the bait.

Every NerdHistory fight

Lake Herman Road

A

Batman wins the dock fight, but it's the wrong man and the killer stays unnamedBatman vs a Gotham gunman with a .22

On December 20, 1968, David Faraday, 17, and Betty Lou Jensen, 16, are parked at a gravel turnout on Lake Herman Road outside Benicia. It is a cold night. Two shots go through the back window and the roof, aimed at nobody. That is how a hunter clears a car. David is shot as he climbs out, and Betty Lou makes it 28 feet before five bullets hit her in the back.

The scene gives Batman almost nothing. There are no usable tire tracks or footprints. What's left is ten .22 casings, and a casing works like a fingerprint on brass. The firing pin dents the rim in its own shape. The breech face stamps its pattern into the metal. The extractor and ejector scratch it on the way out.

The casings were .22 Winchester Super-X, and nobody ever found the gun. So Batman files ten locks and no key, then goes looking for a key. Alfred flags a man with a record, a .22 and a habit of parking on empty roads. Batman tails him across the Gotham docks.

The fight is routine. The man's first shot sparks off a container a hand's width from Batman's cowl. A batarang to the wrist drops the pistol, Batman takes a tire iron on the forearm gauntlet, and a stack of crates ends it in under a minute. In the cave, the test-fired brass shows a firing pin dent of the wrong shape. Wrong gun, wrong guy.

We score it an A for Batman. The docks were easy, but he was guessing, and guessing is dangerous against this killer. A driver found the bodies within minutes and the killer was already gone. He planned his exit before he planned anything else.

The Letters and the Ciphers

B

Batman cracks the cipher but the killer escapes unseenBatman vs the 408-symbol cipher and a killer's decoys

On July 4, 1969, Darlene Ferrin and Michael Mageau are parked at Blue Rock Springs Park in Vallejo. A man walks up with a flashlight and opens fire with a 9mm. Darlene dies and Michael survives. About half an hour later, a man phones Vallejo police from a pay phone two blocks from headquarters. He reports the shooting and claims the Lake Herman Road killings too. Four weeks later, three Bay Area newspapers each get a third of a 408-symbol cipher.

The cipher is homophonic, which is why it's hard. A plain substitution cipher gives one symbol per letter, so the most common symbol is probably E. Here, 54 symbols cover 26 letters and E alone gets seven. Nothing dominates the count, so basic frequency analysis stalls.

Batman follows the path the real solvers took. Donald Harden, a Salinas teacher, and his wife Bettye broke it by betting on words instead of counts. Look for a doubled symbol, because "kill" has a doubled letter. Find a four-symbol cluster that fits. Then bet that a man this vain starts with "I." Batman tries "I like killing," and the page falls.

The message reads like a manifesto: killing people is more fun than hunting wild game, and his victims become his slaves in the afterlife. He also says he won't give his name, which undercuts the letter's promise that his identity is in the cipher. A school teacher and his wife broke his big code within days. It shows how badly he needs to be noticed.

The phone call is the better lead. In Gotham it traces to a booth outside a shuttered diner. A figure runs, and Batman chases him over the rooftops. The runner is no killer. A stranger paid him $200 to stand there and run. A second man steps out of the stairwell and clips Batman's cowl with a pipe. Batman drops him, but the runner is gone. We score it a B: he cracked the code but got played with two decoys and a ringing skull.

Lake Berryessa and the Hooded Killer

A

Batman survives the cult, wins the brawl, and learns the killer isn't among themBatman vs a 40-person hooded cult

On September 27, 1969, Cecelia Shepard and Bryan Hartnell are on a blanket at Lake Berryessa when a man walks out of the trees. He wears a black, square-topped hood with clip-on sunglasses over the eyeholes and a bib with the cross-circle symbol from the letters. He carries a pistol and a long knife and claims to be an escaped convict. He has pre-cut lengths of plastic clothesline. He stabs Bryan six times and Cecelia ten. Cecelia dies two days later, and Bryan survives.

Batman reads the scene as planning. The hood was sewn, not bought. The pre-cut clothesline means the man walked down the slope knowing how many people he'd tie. His footprints say size 10½ Wing Walker, a government-issue military boot used by both the Air Force and the Navy. On Bryan's car door, in black felt-tip, he wrote the cross-circle, "Vallejo," the dates of all three attacks, the time of this one, and "by knife." A man who wants to disappear doesn't write a receipt on his victim's car.

He then phoned it in himself from a Napa car wash a few blocks from the sheriff's office. He was staging it. The hood is part of the show, because a monster gets remembered. Matt Reeves has said the Zodiac was an inspiration for his Riddler in The Batman.

The rope is the lead Batman can run. Synthetic cord carries a signature in its polymer, its diameter and the twist direction of the strands, and a comparison microscope lines them up. Knots are weaker evidence than TV suggests, since most people tie the same everyday overhand knots. The fibers lead to a Gotham hardware supplier, then to a cash buyer who left a flyer for a meeting in a gutted warehouse.

Forty people in hoods and black robes are chanting around a cross-circle painted on the floor. Batman drops through the skylight. A chain wraps his forearm, five or six bodies pin him to a column, and a kitchen knife hunts for the gap under his arm. Forty amateurs can't outfight him, but they can bury him. He cuts the lantern rope with a batarang, and his lenses see in the dark while their eyes don't. The knife finds his gauntlet, not his ribs.

None of them is the killer. Their robes are store-bought, and this killer sews his own. We score it an A: for one bad second the pile had him, but he picked the terrain, killed the lights and walked out on his own feet.

Presidio Heights and the Final Trap

S

BatmanBatman vs the hooded killer on a Gotham rooftop

On October 11, 1969, cab driver Paul Stine picks up a fare in San Francisco. The passenger shoots him once in the head with a 9mm, takes his wallet and keys, and tears off a piece of his bloody shirt. Three teenagers watch from across the street. A patrol car passes a white man walking away. Two days later the Chronicle gets the shirt swatch. The letters turn nasty, with a threat to shoot a school bus tire and pick off the children, a fourth page of symbols, and a seven-page letter with a bomb diagram.

Batman sets a trap in print. He plants a story in the Gotham Gazette saying a witness saw the killer's real face and will identify him, with an address and a time. The killer dismissed the police sketch in his own letter and claimed glue on his fingertips left no prints. He argues with newspapers. This is bait built for a man who can't let a sketch go.

At midnight a figure in a black hood with a white cross-circle waits on the roof and fires first. A brick chimney takes the shot meant for Batman's head. Then the knife opens a gash across Batman's chest. In Batman Begins, Lucius Fox says the suit stops a knife, but real Kevlar mostly won't. Woven fiber catches a bullet by spreading the hit across the weave, and a blade point pushes the threads apart.

Then the man rips open his jacket and shows a bomb with his thumb on the trigger. Batman can't beat that thumb. The average person needs roughly a quarter of a second to react to what they see. Elite athletes get down to around 0.15 to 0.2 seconds, and that still isn't fast enough. The question is whether the man has decided to die. The 340 cipher, cracked in December 2020 after 51 years, has him saying he isn't afraid of the gas chamber. His exits say the opposite. At Lake Herman Road he planned his escape first.

At Lake Berryessa he performed for an audience, and performers pause for the reaction. Batman bets on the exits. The laugh runs half a second long, and Batman drives an elbow into his arm while he's still performing. The arm folds, the hand opens, and Batman rips the trigger loose on its wire.

Now chapter one pays off. The real case has locks everywhere and no key: the brass never matched anyone and the handwriting never matched a suspect. Tonight the tools have a body. Loops are the most common fingerprint pattern, at roughly 52–65% of prints, while whorls run about 27–40% and arches about 4–15%. What identifies a person is the ridge endings and splits inside them. Batman matches the man's prints to the bloody ones in the cab, his printed line to the letters' slant and spacing, and the roof's brass to the cab casing under the same microscope. We score it S: he set the trap, held the roof and walked away from a gun, a knife and a live bomb.

What it comes down to

Batman wins because he reads the man, not because he out-punches him. Chapter 1's brass gives him locks without a key, the cipher shows a killer who needs to be noticed, and the Berryessa hood and clothesline show a performer. Tier S comes from the bait, a Gotham newspaper and a killer vain enough to take it. Those supply the suspect the real investigators never had, so every piece of evidence matches at once. In the real world the man was never named and the case is still open.
