Daredevil vs the Colosseum: Does He Survive?

Blind vigilante Matt Murdock faces four Colosseum fights while 50,000 Romans roar, to test whether his radar sense survives noise, injury, an animal and blood loss.

Daredevil survives all four gates, because nothing in the arena beat his senses.

Every NerdHistory fight

The Net Man

C

DaredevilDaredevil vs the retiarius

The fight turns on the net and the noise. Matt's edge is hearing a heartbeat at 20 feet and the breath that catches before a throw. Marvel's own reference material lists his weakness plainly: excessive noise can temporarily weaken his radar sense. We put 50,000 voices in a roofless stone bowl on top of that, and Matt hasn't learned to filter it yet.

The retiarius has no helmet and almost no armor, and Rome ranked him at the bottom. The poet Juvenal used a nobleman fighting as a net man as his example of a Roman disgracing himself in public. Rome was wrong about the weapon. The net is the one thing on the sand that doesn't have to aim. Reconstructions put it at roughly 10 feet across, with lead weights on the rim and a draw cord tied to the thrower's wrist. You can't dodge a point. You have to be outside the circle.

Matt's first move is the wrong one. He rushes in like it's a knife fight in an alley off Ninth Avenue. The weighted cord wraps his ankle, the cord pulls tight, and he hits the sand hard. The trident comes down, and he rolls a half second ahead of it because he still hears the man's breath hitch. That one sound is his tell.

Once he's on the ground the crowd fades into the background and he finds the man's rhythm: cast, thrust, step back, reset. He stops chasing and waits for the next cast. Then he steps through the gap in the net, grabs the draw cord and hauls. The cord is tied to the man's wrist, so the retiarius stumbles forward into arm's length, where a ranged weapon is useless. Matt strips the trident and drives him to one knee. Then he lets him go. The retiarius raises one finger, the Roman signal for mercy, and the guards take him out.

We give this a C because nothing in the fight came close to killing Matt. It came close to embarrassing him. The difference was one filtered sound.

The Secutor

B

DaredevilDaredevil vs the secutor

The secutor was built as the retiarius's opposite, the most famous pairing in the sport. His helmet is a smooth egg with two eye holes too small for a trident prong, so a net slides off it. The cost, by our figures, is a couple of kilos of bronze (about 4 to 5 pounds) with almost no air inside. The sources agree the secutor had to win fast or faint. That makes his stamina a clock he can't turn off.

He opens with a bull rush behind a shield of roughly 11 to 15 pounds. A big body on a straight line is easy for a man who dodges point-blank gunfire, and Matt just steps off the line. Then he tries the old trick against a bigger man: fake high, go low, take the head. That is the mistake. The helmet has nothing to grip, and the shield boss catches him in the ribs and cracks one. The helmet did its job and made him commit to something that wasn't there.

The Netflix Daredevil show understood this and the 2003 movie never did. Matt is a man who gets hit, and he wins by reading, not by being untouchable. So he stops fighting the shape and listens to the ragged breathing under the bronze.

He uses that breathing as a lever on the whole charge. The secutor's own weight carries him forward and his helmet slams under the rim of his own shield. His grip loosens. Matt wrenches the shield away and has him kneeling with the gladius pointed down. Then he drops the sword in the sand to show he's done. The B is for the one hit he took, because that cracked rib goes with him through every gate after this.

The Beast Hunt

A

DaredevilDaredevil vs a Barbary lion

Our lion is a Barbary lion, the North African kind Rome imported in large numbers for these morning beast hunts, called venationes. Matt's senses do work on animals. He can hear a heartbeat at 20 feet, and a lion that big and slow is easy to place. The problem is that everything he beat that day was a habit. A trained man rehearses his throw, his charge and his breath. A cat goes from still to full commitment with nothing in between.

The numbers explain why. We call it about 400 pounds, twice Matt's weight. We reject the 19th-century claims of roughly 660 pounds as untrustworthy. We put the bite at around 650 PSI. A lion covers 20 feet of sand in about a second, near 50 mph, and its claws run about 1.5 inches. The first pass opens three claw lines in Matt's side that burn worse than anything the net did. He stops trying to predict it.

What he reads instead is the sand. Every paw fall comes up through the wooden arena floor as a signal, and the gap where all four feet leave the ground is the pounce. He digs out the half-buried gladius and moves toward the barrier wall instead of away from the lion. A lion can clear about 36 feet in a bound, so the wall won't stop it. It only tells Matt where the bound ends.

When the lion gathers and leaves the ground, the sand goes quiet, and that silence is his signal. He drops to a knee, braces the pommel on the ground and puts about 20 inches of blade up under the rib cage as the animal comes down. The sword doesn't stop 400 pounds in the air. The lion still lands on him, and for a few seconds it is still a lion. His forearm and the hilt keep its teeth off his throat. Then the breathing stops.

We give this an A. Three claw wounds are the closest anything came to killing him all day. It didn't beat his senses. It never learned a habit for him to read.

The Editor's Champion

S

DaredevilDaredevil vs the dimachaerus

This fight is decided by blood loss as much as by steel. Add up what Matt has left on the sand: the shin, the ribs, three lines down his side. He is somewhere inside what trauma medicine calls a class two hemorrhage, with 15 to 30 percent of his blood gone. His heart rate is 100 to 120 at rest, and his reaction time slows. The read still happens, but the body arrives late.

The dimachaerus is the gladiator historians know least about. There is one inscription from Lyon and a few mosaics that don't agree on the kit. Any writer who puts one in a story is inventing half of him. The sources do agree that fighting with two swords is hard enough that these men were probably among the best in their school. This one doesn't rush. He circles slowly, having watched Matt bleed through three fights, and waits for him to run out.

The first exchange proves the champion right. Both blades come in as a cross cut, and Matt hears it start. His sidestep still lands a quarter second behind his intention, and one edge opens his forearm to the bone. The arena tilts. But he has tracked two blades before. Elektra fights with twin sai, and he has been on the wrong side of them more than once. Two arcs from one body are still one body, and a body has to put its weight somewhere before an arm can move.

So Matt stops fighting like himself and lets the senses run it. He catches the champion's weight rolling onto his front foot to load the next cut and is already moving. He goes inside both blades, closes his good hand on the nearest wrist and twists, and that sword drops. The champion's own momentum carries the second blade forward. Matt turns it and presses the flat against the man's throat. The slow heartbeat up in the editor's box finally runs fast, and the champion yields before Matt has to decide anything.

We give this an S. The champion wasn't better than the lion. Matt beat him with a quarter of his blood on the ground and the one thing blood loss couldn't take, which was the read.

What it comes down to

Our case is that Daredevil wins because the Colosseum never beat his senses. Noise swamped him for one net throw, and a lion with no habits gave him the closest call. Every other fighter telegraphed something: a breath, a weight shift, a charge. Matt reads those tells, so he survives all four gates with a cracked rib, claw wounds and a cut forearm. His tiers climb from C to S as his blood drops.
