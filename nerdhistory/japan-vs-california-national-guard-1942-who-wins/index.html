Japan vs California National Guard, 1942: Who Wins?

A California National Guard sergeant with two 155mm guns and 88 Garand rounds faces a Japanese landing at Santa Barbara in February 1942.

The guardsman gets home because Japan's invasion runs out of fuel first.

Every NerdHistory fight

The Landing

D

Japanese landing force (the beachhead holds and grows)California National Guard battery vs Japanese landing force

The battery loses because it gets one salvo. The crew rams home a 95-pound, 6-inch shell and fires. One round lands short and one lands long, which brackets the target, so the next salvo would hit. Dive bombers arrive first and wipe out the headland. The guns are gone before they can fire for effect.

The carriers are the weakest link in the premise. In real February 1942, Japan's big carriers were putting 188 planes over Darwin, Australia, nearly 7,000 nautical miles from Santa Barbara. The story grants Japan the air cover so it can ask what happens on the ground. The opening is also real: on February 23, 1942, the submarine I-17 shelled the Ellwood oil field for about 20 minutes and hit a derrick and a pump house.

The Garand ping is our first test. The sergeant dumps his half-spent clips to avoid it, which costs four rounds. The Japanese rifleman who rushes him comes the way Japanese infantry were drilled: two men over the berm while a third fires to pin him down. The ping had nothing to do with it. In a 1952 Army study called Project Doughboy, only one in four Korea riflemen objected to the sound. Researcher Jonathan Ferguson writes that there is no evidence the ping ever endangered a rifleman.

We score this a D. The sergeant is unhurt with 48 rounds, but he is cut off behind a growing beachhead. His 40-man battery is down to five or six. Santa Barbara's harbor, railyard and phone exchange are gone. The second wave is rolling steel fuel drums up the sand, and Highway 101 is jammed with fleeing civilians. Watch both.

The Farmhouse

F

Japanese force (only the sergeant gets out)Guardsmen in a farmhouse vs Type 95 tank and Japanese infantry

The Type 95 light tank is a weak machine. It has half an inch of armor at its thickest and a short 37mm gun. Against a house full of men with rifles, that is plenty. Its first shell punches through the porch rail and its second takes out the front window, which sends glass across the room. The guardsmen can shoot the infantry behind it, but they cannot stop the tank.

The fight is decided by a Malaya-style flank. Under General Yamashita, Japanese troops broke British defensive lines down the peninsula with frontal pressure and deep flanking moves through the jungle. Here the Japanese lieutenant parks the tank where every rifle in the house can see it. A machine gun rakes the wall at chest height, and two squads go through the lemon rows toward the kitchen door.

The guardsmen's one hope is artillery, and it is gone. The farmhouse phone line runs through the Santa Barbara exchange, which has already been destroyed, so the call goes nowhere. The lieutenant is shot through the throat while turning from the dead phone. The private beside the sergeant is out of ammo, so the sergeant hands him two clips. That leaves the sergeant 24 rounds.

The kitchen door blows in while every man is watching the tank. This is the same army that bayoneted patients at Alexandra Hospital in Singapore, so the bayonets are no bluff. The sergeant survives with a fireplace poker and a cast-iron skillet, and the other guardsmen are dead. He goes out the pantry window with a wound across his thigh, and we drop his tier to F.

The Fighting Withdrawal

C

The Americans (the bridge crossing is stopped)One 75mm gun and a demolished bridge vs Japanese tanks and infantry

No rescue comes from the sea. In the last week of February 1942, the carrier Enterprise was bombing Wake Island, about 4,000 nautical miles away. The Saratoga was in a shipyard with a Japanese torpedo hole in her side. The sergeant's way out is an army truck convoy, and then he climbs off it to load a 75mm field gun at a river bridge with a 30-round supply.

The 75 does what a field gun at point-blank range should. The first Type 95 takes a hit through the hull and burns. The second tank does not need a shell. It stops 50 yards short of the bridge with its engine coughing, because it is out of fuel. Its drums are 30 miles back behind a highway full of abandoned Packards. A Type 95 can go well over 100 miles on a full load on a good road. This one has ground through orchards for a day and a night.

The third tank brings the cost. The 75 misses, the tank's 37mm shell bursts on the sandbags, and the gunner, who had said "30" rounds, is killed. The sergeant can load the gun but not aim it. An engineer lights the fuses with the tank on the span. The bridge folds and the tank drops into the river nose first.

The tier jumps from F to C. The bridge is down and the tanks are stalling, but the sergeant's ride is gone and he has a single clip. The captain circles a fuel depot three miles behind Japanese lines on a map and gives him demolition charges.

The Counterstrike

A

The Americans (the depot is destroyed)Demolition raiders vs the Japanese fuel depot

The captain circles fuel instead of guns because of how Japan planned the campaign. Its army traveled light and expected to eat and fuel up from captured stores. Troops even had a name for British supplies seized in retreat: Churchill rations. When Yamashita's men took Kuala Lumpur, they found food, fuel and ammunition waiting. In California the only supply is what came ashore in drums, so destroying the drums takes away the army's way to move.

The raid is quiet until it isn't. Twelve men wade the river, blacken their faces with burnt cork and creep through the lemon grove at midnight. A dog barks and a guard steps close, so the sergeant uses a knife instead of the rifle to keep the hollow asleep. He puts charges on a captured gasoline truck and the nearest row of drums. Then he spends his last eight rounds covering two engineers caught in the open. He lets the Garand ping ring, since nothing is left to hide.

We do not oversell the result. Nothing stalls that night, because every tank and truck still holds whatever is in its own tank. The drums go up in a chain, and the army is stranded within a day or two. At dawn, American planes from inland airfields sweep the beachhead. The tier climbs to A: the invaders can still fight for a day or two, but they can't go anywhere.

What it comes down to

The sergeant's 88 rounds were never going to stop an army, and he does not need them to. The invasion fails on supply. Japanese tanks and trucks run on fuel that comes ashore in steel drums, and that fuel is stuck behind a jammed highway, then burned in one depot. The real-world parallel is oil: roughly 80% of Japan's oil imports in the late 1930s came from the United States, and sales continued until the summer of 1941. In the story, Japan's army runs dry on an American coast dotted with derricks. The sergeant lives, but nobody from his battery walks home with him.
