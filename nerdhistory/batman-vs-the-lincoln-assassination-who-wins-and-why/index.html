Batman vs the Lincoln Assassination: Who Wins and Why

Batman enters Ford's Theatre on April 14, 1865, and fights four encounters, from a back corridor to a carriage chase, to stop Booth's conspiracy.

Batman saves Lincoln at a cost, but the wider plot keeps moving.

Every NerdHistory fight

The scout in the back corridors

BatmanBatman vs Atzerodt's hired scout and a paid stagehand

The fight is decided by noise. The corridor is barely 4 feet wide and lined with scenery, so any wide swing hits a flat and brings the cast running. The grapple gun is useless in a space this tight. Batman puts the lookout stagehand down with one short strike to the hinge of the jaw. Knockouts come from rotation, not from a movie neck chop. The head snaps sideways and the lights go out.

The scout's best moment is a stage sandbag. Theaters like this flew scenery on rope with sandbags as counterweights, and a full bag is a serious weight. Swung at a man, it is a club on a rope. Batman could slip it, but slipping means stepping into the flats. So he takes it on the shoulder. Then he steps inside the arc, where the bag has no speed yet, and wraps the rope around the scout's forearm.

One pull locks the wrist and the pry bar drops. Batman catches it before it can clang and pins the scout to the wall. A joke lands on stage and the house laughs, and both men freeze until it fades. The scout gives up the name Atzerodt. In real history George Atzerodt was a German-born carriage painter who ferried Confederate agents across the Potomac. Batman ties the scout up. The costs are a bruised shoulder, a split knuckle and one stagehand who saw the cowl, with no alarm raised.

The side exit: the silent takedown and the lantern signal

B

BatmanBatman vs the man with the revolver and dark lantern

The fight has to be silent. About 1,700 people are in the building, and one gunshot or scream sends them all for the exits. Consider 1913, when a false cry of fire in a Michigan hall killed 73 people, most of them children. The armed man can't fire either, because a shot now would wreck the real plan. So he swings the lantern like a club. Batman clips its handle with a batarang and tears it out of his hand.

Batman then uses the silent takedown that Arkham players know. A blood choke squeezes the carotid arteries and takes several seconds to put someone out. Silent is slow, so Batman takes the slow, quiet option. Those seconds are long enough for the man to slam him into the edge of a stone step and drive an elbow into his side. Something in Batman's ribs gives, and he keeps the choke on.

The brick wall is why nobody upstairs hears it. It turns a fistfight into dull thumps that sound like scenery being moved. A gunshot is far louder and would get through. The man's last kick of the lantern was the signal, a burst of light thrown across the alley wall. Batman ends the fight with a cracked rib and a signal already sent.

Batman vs John Wilkes Booth in the presidential box

Batman (Lincoln lives)Batman vs John Wilkes Booth

Booth is waiting for the biggest laugh in the play. A pistol shot in a packed house needs 1,700 people roaring to cover it. Batman works that out after the side exit and takes the stairs three at a time. He reaches the door as Booth wedges it shut with a broken music stand leg. In history that bar held off rescuers. Tonight Booth gets one second to set it, which isn't enough, and Batman's shoulder knocks it away.

The box is smaller than it looks from the seats. Batman clamps both hands on Booth's wrist and drives it straight up. The gun is a Philadelphia Deringer, 6 inches long and 8 ounces, loaded from the front and firing a .44-caliber lead ball. At 4 feet, nobody dodges it. But the barrel only has to move a few degrees at the start for the ball to hit the ceiling instead of a skull. The shot goes into the plaster and Lincoln never turns around.

Booth draws a big knife. He is 5 foot 8, lean, and known for stage sword fights and acrobatic leaps. Batman pins him to the back wall because the railing is the danger. If Booth gets there, he jumps and runs. If Batman is knocked into it, the crowd sees a black shape in the box. The first slash skids off the gauntlet. The second goes above it and opens Batman's left arm, much like Booth's wound to Major Rathbone in the real history.

Batman rips a heavy curtain off its rod and throws it over the knife arm. The blade tangles, and he twists the bundle until the elbow can't follow and the knife drops. Rathbone pulls Lincoln below the railing, and nobody is hit. We put this just short of S tier because the knife drew blood. The cost was a slashed arm, one ball in the plaster, and a crowd starting to look up.

The man behind the man: the carriage chase and the Johnson and Seward plot

A

BatmanBatman vs the Watcher and the wider conspiracy

Booth tells Batman the pistol was only one piece. That links three facts: a scout who was paid and told nothing, a lantern throwing light into the alley, and an assassin saying there's more than one gun. The signal wasn't a runner's call. It was a start for whatever else happens tonight. Batman hands Booth to two army officers, tells them to get Lincoln down the back stairs, and takes Booth's own exit over the railing, a drop of about 12 feet.

He lands at about 19 mph and rolls to spread the impact. He hits the alley with one eye shut. Eyes need a long time to adapt to the dark, and light undoes that fast, so the shut eye keeps its night vision. In real history Booth's spur caught a flag on this same jump and he broke his left leg.

A galloping horse is faster than any human sprinter, and Batman has a cracked rib. Cutting each corner on the diagonal, about 30% shorter than running both sides, keeps him close for a block. It doesn't win the race. Out here there is finally room for the grapple gun. With his left arm cut he can't climb hand over hand, so the winch reels him in. He pins the Watcher and hauls back on the reins until the horse stops.

The Watcher's coat holds a wax-sealed letter in a cipher. Under the code, in plain ink, are the names Andrew Johnson and William Seward. In real history Atzerodt's job was Johnson, and he lost his nerve at a hotel bar. Seward's attacker didn't. We grade the night A overall, and the grade fits. Lincoln lives and the network has a paper trail. The plot was already moving, so A buys Batman a longer night.

What it comes down to

Batman wins each fight by solving the problem in front of him: noise, crowd panic, the laugh Booth planned around, and the speed of a horse. He keeps every fight quiet until the box, and in the box he wins because Booth's shot is only half a second from being aimed. Tier A means Lincoln lives and the network is exposed, but the plot against Johnson and Seward was already underway, and Batman is hurt.
