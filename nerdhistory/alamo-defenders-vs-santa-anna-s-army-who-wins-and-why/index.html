Alamo Defenders vs Santa Anna's Army: Who Wins and Why

A Mexican lieutenant leads 30 men through the 1836 Alamo siege, scored by survival tier at each chapter using Mexican officers' own accounts.

The lieutenant lives with 16 of 30 men, and the Alamo falls anyway.

The Flag Nobody Should Have Raised

D

Alamo defenders; the lieutenant pulls back with 28 menLieutenant's 30 men vs the Alamo's guns and long rifles

The siege opens with a refusal. A Mexican bugler calls for a parley, and the answer is a shot from the 18-pounder, the biggest gun in the Alamo, on the southwest corner. Santa Anna won't see Jim Bowie's envoy or Travis's, and both get the same reply: no terms, surrender at discretion. Travis wrote the next day that he had answered the demand with a cannon shot, and the Alamo's flag still waves from the walls.

The deciding fight comes on February 25. Santa Anna leads a few hundred men, your battalion, Matamoros, among them, into empty huts 90 to 100 yards from the wall to build a gun battery. Range is the problem. Your men carry the India pattern musket, a cheaper British copy of the Brown Bess with a smooth barrel. Past about 100 yards, firing it is mostly hoping.

Crockett's men and other Texans carry rifles, whose spiral grooves spin the ball so it flies straight. For them, 100 yards is an ordinary shot. Then the eight-pounders open with grapeshot and the mud-and-stick huts come apart. Texans run out with torches to burn your cover. You pull back, give up the battery site and lose a second man to a ball through the arm. Colonel Almonte logged two Mexicans dead and six wounded, while Texan accounts mention only a few scratches from flying rock. That lopsided count is why this chapter scores a D.

The Wall That Shouldn't Hold

C

Santa Anna gets his assault; the lieutenant leaves 4 recruits behind and climbs to C with 24 menSanta Anna's assault order vs the Alamo's walls and a waiting game

A norther drops the temperature to 39 degrees by Almonte's thermometer, and Travis orders his gunners to stop firing and save powder. Almonte's journal records more than once that the enemy did not reply, except now and then. Meanwhile people get in. On March 1, 32 men from Gonzales slip through the Mexican lines. Two days later James Bonham rides through the gate in daylight, with a white handkerchief in his hat so his own side will know him.

That is why Santa Anna chooses to assault instead of waiting. Several senior officers, including Colonel Romero, who will lead your column, argue for opening a breach first and waiting on two 12-pounders still on the road, the guns that could do it. The walls are no paper fence: at least 2.75 feet thick, with a log-and-earth cribbing along the north wall. Help has reached the fort twice, and every night of waiting is another chance for more. The order comes down on March 5: ladders, crowbars, axes.

Your column gets the east wall and six ladders. The order also tells recruits who are deficient in instruction to stay in quarters, so you leave four of your 28 men who have barely fired a musket. That drops you to 24 but keeps frightened boys with loaded muskets out of the dark. The tier rises from D to C because a night attack takes the long rifle's best trick, distance, out of the fight. The 18-pounder, still rationing powder, stays quiet.

The Dark Before the Bugle

F

The defenders' fire; Travis falls and the lieutenant drops to F with 18 menRomero's column vs the Alamo's volleys, canister and north wall

The columns move out after midnight on March 6, silent, and lie flat in the cold within musket shot of the walls. Then the cheers of "Viva Santa Anna" and the bugles break the silence, and the Alamo wakes. Your orders say to open fire while you're still out of range. You tell your men to hold, because firing now means reaching the wall with empty barrels. The cost is walking into Texan volleys without answering. Mexican accounts note that untrained recruits in the columns fired blindly and hit the men in front of them, so the call has a reason.

Travis can't match your numbers, so he multiplies his men instead: by de la Peña's report, three or four guns are stacked beside each defender. The Texans are short on canister, so they pack the cannon with nails, door hinges and chopped-up horseshoes, which turns each gun into a giant shotgun. A three-gun battery hits your column's left flank and kills two men. The next blast takes a ladder and the four men carrying it. At the north wall, one blast takes out half a company of Toluca light infantry, and the next wounds Colonel Duque in the thigh.

The ladder picture is wrong. Few ladders reach the walls. Instead the north wall itself becomes the way up: a stretch of it had been repaired with a log cribbing packed with earth, and the logs work as a staircase. At its foot, the Texan cannon can't be depressed far enough to reach you, so defenders have to lean out over the top to shoot. That's when Travis is hit. Bayonets still throw the first climbers back. Halfway up, a splinter opens your cheek and a hand hauls you down. C drops to F.

The Last Room

S

The Mexican army takes the Alamo; the lieutenant survives with 16 menMexican reserve and the lieutenant's men vs the long barracks and church

The jam at the north wall holds while Santa Anna's reserve piles in. Then General Amador goes up the wall with survivors of the Toluca Battalion and the men around him follow. Inside, the defenders fall back to the long barracks and the church. Mexican troops take the Texan guns on the walls, the 18-pounder from day one among them, and turn captured cannon on the barracks doors.

Once the doors go, the fight is point-blank. A white cloth comes out on a bayonet, but the order on the Mexican side is no quarter, and Sánchez Navarro noted that only the women and children were saved. You take the surrender anyway and send two men through a door. A defender who never meant to give up fires from the back of the room, and one of your two doesn't return. That leaves 16. Smoke and crowding make it worse, with blind fire from untrained recruits hitting their own side.

Inside a room about 15 feet wide, nobody has range, so whoever fires first lives, and in the dark that's mostly luck. A Texan with an empty rifle and a pistol aims at your chest. Neither of you speaks until someone behind you fires and the moment breaks. Texans who flee over the walls run into the lancers Santa Anna posted for that purpose. Ramírez y Sesma reported a skirmish with about 50 Texans outside the walls, a figure the historian Edmondson thinks he inflated. The church falls last, to one more shot from the 18-pounder at the barricade across its door.

De la Peña counted seven prisoners brought before Santa Anna, who orders them killed, and he wrote that one was David Crockett. That line is a big reason his manuscript was called a forgery, but a 2001 study by archivist David Gracy found its paper and ink typical of the Mexican army in the 1830s and its handwriting matching other documents, though historians still argue. The S tier rests on the outcome: you're alive, with 16 men and a cheek wound, when the plaza goes quiet.

Closing: The Count and San Jacinto

S

Mexico takes the Alamo at heavy cost, then loses at San JacintoSanta Anna's army vs the cost of the victory

The lieutenant went in with 24 men and 16 are still standing. The army's cost was larger. Staff officer Sánchez Navarro listed 110 soldiers dead and 247 wounded, not counting officers. He wrote that with another victory like this, they would go to the devil. That line shows how the Mexican side counted the morning, as a win that cost more than it should have.

Six weeks later at San Jacinto, Sam Houston's army broke Santa Anna's in 18 minutes, by Houston's own report, with "Remember the Alamo" as its rallying cry. We end by asking which call mattered most: leaving the recruits in camp, holding fire in the dark, or trusting the white cloth.

What it comes down to

The Alamo fell because Santa Anna chose speed and numbers over a slow siege, and the defenders made every foot cost something. Long rifles, canister packed with scrap, and a wall that gave no ground until it became a staircase meant the lieutenant's tier fell with each chapter and ended at S only because he lived. The Mexican officers' own accounts show a victory the army itself found hard to celebrate.
