Star Wars Battalion vs Vietnam War: Who Wins?

We drop one Imperial battalion with AT-ATs and AT-STs into Vietnam to see whether armor, walkers and blasters beat tunnels, tanks and supply roads.

No: the Empire wins every fight but loses the supply road.

The Jungle Ambush

B

Imperial battalion, at a costImperial battalion vs Viet Cong ambush

The first shot hits a lead trooper in the chest and he sits back up. On screen one blaster bolt drops a stormtrooper, so people assume the armor is paper. It isn't. Canon says the white plastoid stops projectiles from crude slugthrowers, and a 7.62 mm rifle round is exactly that. The sniper sees it fail and slides back into a spider hole.

The AT-STs answer at once. These two-legged scouts stand about 30 feet tall and their chin cannons reach more than a mile, so they flatten the tree line. It looks decisive, but the Viet Cong are already in tunnels under the blast zone. Soil soaks up shock. The real Cu Chi tunnels zigzagged at corners between 60 and 120 degrees, so a blast dies at the next turn. Some complexes ran three levels, the lowest roughly 30 to 40 feet down. The people under the clearing are rattled, not dead.

The jungle then wears the squad down. Bamboo stakes skid off shin plates until one finds the black body glove behind a knee. A trip wire drops a grenade and a trench wall buries a squad. Rain and wet leaves scatter infrared, so helmet optics built for darkness and smoke lose range. Then three RPG teams fire together. An RPG-7 warhead can punch through about 10 inches of steel, and one hits an AT-ST's leg joint. The walker lurches into a tree, its gyros fighting to keep it upright.

The commander stops feeding squads into the trees and calls up an AT-AT. In the canon novel Twilight Company, AT-AT fire hits Echo Base's command center and the corridor to it collapses. Here it collapses the shallow tunnels and buries the hatches under tons of dirt. The deep levels survive, but the ambush doesn't. We score it a B. They broke the ambush, but they lost the initiative, some troopers and a damaged walker, paying for lessons the enemy already knew. It's also the Ewoks' log trap on Endor played straight, with rockets.

The Compound Assault

C

Imperial battalion, barelyImperial garrison vs Viet Cong and NVA night assault

The assault comes at 0200 under parachute flares: hundreds of Viet Cong and NVA regulars crossing open ground toward the wire. The first wave drops in seconds as blaster bolts fold the front ranks, and return rifle fire sparks off chest plates. Stormtroopers are precise, as Obi-Wan says in A New Hope, and the Death Star misses were staged, as Leia says. But the second wave is already stepping over the first, then the third.

The number that decides the night is ammunition. The old Legends spec sheets give the standard E-11 rifle about 100 shots per power cell. A trooper firing fast empties one in a minute or two. Every bit of energy that doesn't leave the muzzle as a bolt stays behind as heat. Metal doesn't glow until it gets extremely hot, and then only dull red. By the third wave, barrels down the line are glowing and creeping toward orange.

Then mortars land inside the wire and walk toward the east perimeter, tearing a fence section flat. An NVA squad pours through and the fight turns into a brawl. Canon says the armor handles most threats but gives reduced protection against blunt weapons. A blind monk with a wooden staff drops troopers in Rogue One. A rifle butt cracks a helmet, men go down into trenches, and hands hunt for the seams at the neck and armpits. A trooper with an empty rifle has lost his precision edge.

An AT-AT that spent the night being rushed forward finally arrives, crushing a supply shed and sweeping the gap with its chin cannons. The last 15 or so NVA hug the inner wall, too close for the cannons to depress. That's why canon gives AT-ATs AT-ST escorts to cover their flanks. An AT-ST steps around the corner and cuts the squad down in front of the command post. We score it a C. They held the compound only because a walker arrived at the right moment, with half the squads out of power or close to it.

The Fortress Assault

D

Imperial battalion, narrowlyImperial battalion vs NVA hillside fortress with T-54 tanks

The troopers fast-rope into the valley and the AT-ATs walk up the valley floor, then stop at the foot of the hill. Canon lists steep hillsides and deep swamps as terrain an AT-AT can't cross, and much of Vietnam is hills and mountains. The walkers fire from the valley floor, but most defenders sit about 20 feet down, the depth of the second level at Cu Chi. The bolts can only dig them out a layer at a time.

The fortress hits back. Soviet T-54s fire from dug-in pits and anti-aircraft guns drop their barrels flat. Their rounds spark off the walkers, so the gunners switch to a transport hovering over the landing zone. A burst through the cockpit rolls it onto its side. That's real history: in the Vietnam War, North Vietnam's light anti-aircraft artillery downed more American aircraft than all its other weapons systems combined.

The tanks then go after the lead AT-AT, and where they aim is the point. Canon says only the heaviest turbolaser weaponry can punch through AT-AT plating, and a 100 mm tank round about 4 inches across can't. But the Rebels worked this out on Hoth: the armor is too strong for blasters, so go for the legs. A leg joint is a hinge where armor thins to leave room for movement. A shaped charge fires a high-speed jet of metal and only needs the hinge. The knee buckles, and the tall machine falls on its soft valley floor.

That walker was the command walker, carrying the long-range array, the way General Veers ran Hoth from inside his. Then a mortar cracks the commander's wristcom, so the battalion has lost its line to orbit. Snipers aim for the neck seals and knee joints, and the red clay from the first morning still rings every gap in the white plastoid. Then the NVA counterattack and the tanks leave their pits. In the open, the second AT-AT picks them off one by one and the surviving AT-ST rakes the infantry. The charge breaks about 45 yards out. We score it a D: they took the fortress, but lost a walker, a transport and their link to orbit.

Holding the Country

D

The roads beat the battalionImperial battalion vs the Vietnamese road war

Conquering a country with one battalion doesn't work on the numbers. A single battalion is a city-sized garrison at best, and at the peak of the war the US had hundreds of thousands of troops in Vietnam and couldn't hold the country either. Every rifleman in the province has also learned the first shooter's lesson: don't shoot the white parts. A single shot to a neck seal drops a point man from a hillside nobody ever finds.

Then supply starts to fail. A mine takes out a supply vehicle and the crew carries the cargo off by hand. Someone counts the power cells in the crates and the number is lower than yesterday's. A replacement transport circles in the storm, takes tracer fire and pulls away. Every power cell, spare part and ration rides in on something that can be hit. The Empire's whole fighting strength lives inside the convoy, and you can't armor 250 miles of road when the enemy needs only one bad curve and one buried charge.

The deciding moment is a convoy boxed in by two mines, one under the lead vehicle and one under the last, on a road barely wide enough for the column. The last AT-AT and AT-ST are miles away, and even at the roughly 37 mph top speed listed for an AT-AT, that's minutes the convoy doesn't have. Three troopers with near-empty rifles hold a burning vehicle while NVA assault teams close in, aiming low for the seams. It's Leia's line to Tarkin in A New Hope: the tighter the grip, the more slips through your fingers.

The AT-AT does break through at a dead run, as close to a run as a walker gets, and rakes both sides of the road until the assault teams scatter. But the battalion survives only to request the next convoy. We score it a D overall. It won every fight it was handed and lost the road, and the road is where armies like this one die.

What it comes down to

One Imperial battalion can win battles. Plastoid armor stops rifle rounds, and walkers wreck tree lines, bunkers and tanks. But it can't win the war. Tunnels survive walker fire, power cells run dry, AT-AT legs have hinges, and the troopers' white armor has seams. The decisive weakness is the supply road. The battalion's strength lives in convoys that mines and ambushes can hit, and the same road has beaten much larger armies in the real war. We score the battalion a D.
