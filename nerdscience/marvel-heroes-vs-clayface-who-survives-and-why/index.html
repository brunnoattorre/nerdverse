Marvel Heroes vs Clayface: Who Survives and Why

Five Marvel heroes enter a Gotham warehouse one at a time to face Clayface, a villain who wins by smothering, not striking, and we score each survival.

Only Iceman stops Clayface, because cold attacks what he's made of.

Every NerdScience fight

Luke Cage: the wall that doesn't break

C

Clayface (Cage escapes)Luke Cage vs Clayface

Cage's skin does what it always does, and it doesn't matter. His opening hook sinks about 2 inches into Clayface's face and meets no cartilage and no bone. Five more punches vanish the same way. Strength isn't the problem. The handbooks have him lifting anywhere from 5 to 50 tons, and at the middle number he has punched through 4 inches of steel plate. Steel breaks. Clayface doesn't.

The attack was never aimed at his hide. Clayface wraps Cage's wrists, then his throat, and the mask climbs over his jaw and nose and seals his mouth. Unbreakable skin spreads a bullet's force out until it can't do damage. Smothering isn't force, it's denial. A full lungful is about 5 liters of air, which holds roughly a quart of oxygen, and a man working this hard burns through it many times faster than at rest, when the body uses only about a quarter liter a minute. That leaves seconds, not minutes, before his brain starts negotiating.

Cage does get out. He rips both arms apart in one burst and the torn chunks slap the concrete, but they crawl back and refold into a whole body in under 3 seconds. Force works on things that stay broken, and Clayface doesn't stay anything. The tear buys one breath, and Cage spends it on the wall. We score it a C: he walks out on his own, but his invulnerability never entered the fight.

Iron Fist: no pressure points

B

Clayface (Danny retreats)Iron Fist vs Clayface

Danny Rand's whole style is anatomy. He drives gold-charged strikes into the brachial plexus in the side of the neck, the joint behind the knee and the base of the skull. Each one lands clean and does nothing, because Clayface has no nerve in that neck and no joint behind that knee. The fist sinks in, displaces mass, and the mass folds back around his wrists. There's no skeleton to lever against and no nervous system to overload.

So Danny stops aiming and starts hitting, pouring chi into raw force straight through center mass. The punch blows a crater clean through Clayface. Output was never his problem. In New Avengers #59, his chi-charged chop on the floor of a H.A.M.M.E.R. helicarrier brings the whole thing down. Danny is out of his depth on target, not on power. The crater costs Clayface about a second and a half.

Then Clayface splits down the middle, one half peeling off as a second body, and Danny is flanked on a roof with no retreat. A hardened mud fist clips his ribs while he blocks the other side. He does the math and moves his chi into his legs. He runs rooftop to rooftop for three blocks. Clayface can stretch but can't sprint, so he can't close the gap. We score it a B: bruised ribs, a drained tank, a clear head.

Captain America: the shapeshifting mirror

A

Clayface (Cap leaves untouched)Captain America vs Clayface

Steve Rogers is the only hero who arrives with a file. He has Cage's account of the smothering grip and Danny's notes on the split and the flank, so he knows two things that failed. He opens with the shield up, testing range, circling at an angle, letting a mud fist glance off the rim, and mapping Clayface's reach and recovery time. It works for about 90 seconds.

Then the read backfires. Clayface throws the same overhand twice. Steve clocks the timing and steps inside the counter, but Clayface throws the counter first, with the same footwork and angle, and his arm has flattened into a rough copy of Steve's shield mid-swing. That fits the character. Basil Karlo debuted in Detective Comics #40 in June 1940 as an actor, and mimicry was the act before it was the power. Every read Steve takes becomes something Clayface can wear half a second later.

You can't out-fight a mirror, so Steve stops trading and uses the alley. He drives the fight up a rusted fire escape, which forces Clayface to climb instead of flow. Clayface's best moment comes here: a tendril clamps Steve's boot to the third landing and starts up his shin for two seconds. Steve shears it off with the shield rim. The shield is the comic version, a vibranium-steel alloy with a catalyst nobody has reproduced. Names like Molecule Man or Thanos with a gauntlet have broken it. Nothing in this alley is on that list.

What matters is where he throws it. A water tower on the roof is corroded at the base. One throw buckles a leg, and 10,000 gallons, roughly 40 tons, lands on Clayface as he hauls himself over the parapet. Water doesn't dissolve a healthy Clayface, but 40 tons smears a skeletonless body across a roof, and a whole spread-out body takes longer to reform than a chunk. Steve uses that window to leave. It's an A, because he read Clayface and left before the read stopped mattering.

Wolverine and Iceman: regeneration meets the freeze

WO

Iceman (Clayface frozen solid)Wolverine and Iceman vs Clayface

Logan skips the file. Monsters are monsters, so he tracks the scent to an East Side parking garage and pops his claws. The first pass goes as expected: adamantium goes through Clayface's shoulder like a wire through wet clay, and every cut closes before the follow-through finishes. Cage's tears at least left chunks. Logan's cuts leave nothing, because it's like carving water. After the fourth strike Clayface stops giving ground and pours into Logan's open mouth, down his throat and into his lungs.

This is an internal problem, and claws can't touch what's already inside you. The healing factor repairs tissue, but it doesn't make oxygen. Marvel has put it on the page that Logan can be drowned. The adamantium on his skeleton makes him sink, and X-Force #50 shows him in a diving suit for exactly that reason. His healing factor keeps repairing oxygen-starved tissue as it goes, so his clock runs longer than a human's, but it still runs. He's alive because of the healing factor, out because of his anatomy, and saved because someone else showed up. That's a D.

Bobby Drake arrives two minutes behind, already iced over, because someone back home read the file even if Logan wouldn't. He can't freeze all of Clayface while part of it is inside Logan's chest, so he goes for the base. Cold floods the legs and the floor around them. Clayface does what he's done all night and pulls his mask back to the core. The tendril rips out of Logan's throat and Logan hits the concrete, breathing.

Clayface's one swing is a hardened mace of an arm that cracks against Bobby's ice shell. Bobby freezes the arm mid-recoil, then the rest of him. Clayface flows because his mass is fluid, and fluids flow because molecules slide past each other. Cold stops the sliding. Marvel's character profiles have Iceman able to drop his body temperature to absolute zero (about -459°F), and he needs nowhere near that. A body that can't flow can't reshape, stretch or attack. Batman freezes him with Mr. Freeze's grenades in Arkham City, and the 2004 cartoon The Batman used freeze pellets. Iceman gets the S.

What it comes down to

Durability, chi, strategy and a healing factor all answer force, and Clayface never uses any. He takes your air, and he can't be broken or out-read for long. Cold is the only power on the list that attacks what he's made of, not what shape he's in. That's why the hero nobody would have sent first is the one who stops him, though the cracks in that ice won't hold forever.
