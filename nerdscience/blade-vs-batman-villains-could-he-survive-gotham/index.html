Blade vs Batman Villains: Could He Survive Gotham?

We drop post-Morbius comics Blade into Gotham with six serum vials and test his vampire toolkit against five villains who share nothing with vampires.

Blade survives until sunrise, but never beats anyone on their own terms.

Every NerdScience fight

Killer Croc in the Sewers

B

Blade, by collapsing the tunnelBlade vs Killer Croc

Blade's best move is a silver stake into the shoulder seam, and Croc plucks it out like a splinter. A stake only kills a vampire because it's a species-specific kill, not a trauma weapon. In anyone else it's a 6-inch hole, and silver is just an expensive metal to a man who isn't allergic to it.

The sword fares no better. Croc's hide carries osteoderms, bony plates grown into the skin under the scales, and real crocodilian armor is hard enough to turn a bullet at a shallow angle. Behind it sits a jaw built on the saltwater crocodile's record bite, measured at 3,700 pounds of force on a 17-foot animal. We run the modern Croc, not the skin-condition strongman of his 1983 debut.

Blade is faster, but an 8-foot-wide tunnel gives him a gutter and a wall, and speed only pays when you have room to spend it. Croc backhands him through a brick pillar, breaking two ribs, then drags him under for the death roll. A body in water can't brace against rotation. Blade gets a thumb into an eye, the one gap the armor can't close.

He then attacks the building instead of the animal. A stake levers loose rusted iron pipe junctions and the ceiling comes down on both of them, snapping his femur. The healing costs two vials, leaving four. It's a B because he walks out under his own power. He didn't beat Croc. He beat Gotham's plumbing.

Scarecrow in the Fear Toxin Warehouse

A

BladeBlade vs Scarecrow

Scarecrow never throws a punch. His toxin is an inhaled hallucinogen that turns a victim's worst fears into things they see and feel. It doesn't care how fast you are, because it never touches your body. It attacks the circuit that decides whether to run.

Blade's biology doesn't help here. His half-vampire blood is built to shrug off physical damage, but fear toxin isn't really a poison. It's a signal, and his brain is still wired like a human's. The ghosts come: a nest in Prague, a girl he didn't reach, and a vampire wearing his mother's face. He swings, and his sword sticks 8 inches into a support column.

Crane's second canister, point blank at the back of Blade's neck, is the best hit anyone lands on him all night. It fails because Blade has already met his worst memory and had 40 years to make peace with it. The ghosts are loud, but they're old. His hands shake and he keeps moving.

Blade stops trusting his eyes. He sets off a flashbang at his own feet, roughly 170 dB, and his hearing goes to a flat ring. Crane needed to see, and now he can't. Blade tracks burlap and old sweat by nose, throws a stake blind and pins Crane's sleeve to a steel beam. That's an A: scratched, no vial spent. Scarecrow, who debuted in 1941 as a costumed professor of fear, only wins if he lands the first dose on a stranger.

Man-Bat 100 Feet Over Gotham

C

Blade, barely, thanks to a billboardBlade vs Man-Bat

Man-Bat takes the fight into the air, where every Blade technique fails. Blade's whole style is built on pushing against the ground, and 100 feet up his legs are cargo. Man-Bat works like a hawk on a rabbit: climb, release, swoop, slash on the pass.

Then the screech starts. Real bat calls run above 20 kHz and can hit around 140 dB at the source. Bats survive their own voices by contracting a middle ear muscle on every pulse. Man-Bat is protected from his weapon and Blade isn't. His enhanced hearing becomes a funnel into his skull, and the balance organs share bone with the hearing organs. He loses which way is up.

Blade stops fighting the vertigo and spends it. When Man-Bat flares wide for the finishing dive, he puts a silver stake through the wing. A bat's wing is skin stretched over elongated finger bones, thin because every extra ounce costs lift. It tears like wet canvas and one side loses lift.

They fall six stories into a billboard, and the scaffold eats most of a 60-foot drop. A clean drop from that height would mean impact near 40 mph onto the street, and Blade is a man who gets badly hurt by gunfire, not one who walks that off. He lands on top and heaves Man-Bat into a girder. It costs him another vial, and it's a C. He survives because a billboard was in the way.

Solomon Grundy in the Cemetery

F

Nobody. Blade escapes and Grundy can't be killedBlade vs Solomon Grundy

This is the worst grade of the night, and Grundy never lands a clean punch. Blade hunts the dead, so a corpse out of cemetery mud feels like home. But vampires are corpses that still run on biology, and Grundy runs on a swamp. He has no heart to stake and no blood to drain. Two stakes do nothing, and a sword through the neck doesn't slow him.

Grundy made Green Lantern's ring useless in his debut, and nothing in Blade's documented durability survives a clean hit. So it's 40 seconds of pure evasion, and Blade is quick enough to manage it. Grundy is a walking haymaker with no footwork. One graze still throws Blade 20 feet into a headstone and breaks his collarbone.

Grundy's defining trait, since his 1944 debut in All-American Comics #61, is that he comes back. Destroy him and he rises from the swamp again, sometimes smarter, sometimes stronger. He began as a Green Lantern problem. You can't write a win against him, only a delay.

So Blade backs into a stone crypt and kicks out the support beam. Several tons of marble buy him minutes, and that's all the F means. Blade runs out of blood over a long night and Grundy runs out of nothing. The fifth vial goes on the collarbone, leaving one.

Mr. Freeze at the Ice Rink

D

BladeBlade vs Mr. Freeze

Freeze's cold gun freezes Blade's thigh in under a second, and the damage goes deeper than the frost. Cold triggers vasoconstriction, so the body pulls blood out of the limbs to protect the core. Muscles with no blood stop answering, and nerve signals slow with every degree lost.

Blade's healing factor is a circulation-powered process that runs on blood delivered fast to the damage. Freeze isn't attacking the leg. He's attacking the supply line to the repair. The punches come late, ice climbs the ribs, and the cemetery limp becomes a stagger. The armored suit is stronger than the man inside, and Freeze doesn't need to be fast. He needs Blade to be slow.

Blade finds the one published weakness he's handed all night. Victor Fries can't survive outside sub-zero conditions, which is why he lives in the suit. That condition comes from the 1992 Batman: The Animated Series episode "Heart of Ice" by Paul Dini, not the 1959 comic gimmick villain Mr. Zero in Batman #121.

Blade throws his sword at a steam valve on the rink's old refrigeration housing. The pipe bursts and vapor well over 300 degrees Fahrenheit floods a suit built to keep its occupant below 32. The face plate crazes, alarms whine, and Freeze retreats into the fog clutching his chest. It's a D. Blade wins, but he arrived with one vial left and nearly died.

What it comes down to

Blade's whole method is diagnosis: name the monster, find the weakness, use it. Gotham's villains run on armor, brain chemistry, sonar, undeath and thermodynamics, and none of them has a silver entry. So Blade never wins on his own terms. He beats Croc and Freeze by turning the building or the machinery against them, outlasts Scarecrow with 40 years of old ghosts, and survives Man-Bat only because of a billboard. Grundy can only be delayed. He walks out at sunrise with six empty vials and four enemies who now know his face.
