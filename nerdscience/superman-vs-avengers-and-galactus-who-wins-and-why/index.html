Superman vs Avengers and Galactus: Who Wins and Why

Superman crash-lands in Marvel's Manhattan and climbs from Spider-Man and Luke Cage to Thor, Hulk and Galactus, with a survival tier after every round.

Galactus wins; Superman survives at tier D, pulled out by the Silver Surfer.

Every NerdScience fight

Street Level

A

Superman, by restraint (barely scratched, off balance)Superman vs Spider-Man and Luke Cage

Superman wins this round by holding back, not by hitting hard. He's groggy from the crash, so Spider-Man's webbing blinds him for a moment. He forgets he can see through it with his vision. The comics put the web's breaking point around 77,000 pounds per square inch, which is structural-steel territory. He rips it off anyway.

Touching Spider-Man is the hard part. Superman can hear his heartbeat from a mile away, but Spider-Man lifts about 10 tons, and one careless grab could put him through a building. So Superman keeps his speed way down. Spider-Man's spider sense warns him half a second before each grab, and he twists away every time.

Luke Cage tests the restraint. Superman throws a calibrated punch about as hard as Cage's own best, the kind that has gone through 4 inches of steel plate. Cage slides about 3 feet and looks annoyed. Cage debuted in 1972 and is now rated around 25 tons, with skin as hard as titanium steel. His skin doesn't stop the push. It stops the puncture, because the force spreads across dense tissue, so nothing tears. The momentum still has to go somewhere, which is why he slides 3 feet instead of flying 30.

Then the two of them gang up. Cage bear-hugs Superman through a storefront while Spider-Man webs his legs. Superman could plant his feet and stop Cage cold, but that would snap Cage's arms. Once he sees they only want to keep him from hurting anyone, he sets Cage against a wall and pins Spider-Man to the brick with one hand. He stays at tier A: barely scratched, but off balance in a universe that doesn't play by his rules.

The Avengers

A

SupermanSuperman vs Iron Man and Captain America

Tony Stark keeps files on how to stop a hero who goes off the rails, but none cover Superman. So the armor builds one live. Superman dials his heat vision way down because there's a man inside, and the armor eats it. Stark's suits have soaked up energy attacks for decades. The first pulse scorches the plating, the second barely warms it, and the third does nothing. In a blink, about a third of a second, the armor runs billions of calculations.

Tony's best trick is a sonic burst aimed at a man who can hear a heartbeat a mile away. That weakness comes from 1986's The Dark Knight Returns, where Batman's sonic weapon gave Superman a nosebleed. Superman staggers and his vision swims. But in that story the sonic was only part of the setup. A kryptonite arrow turned the fight, and this universe has no kryptonite for the second half of the plan.

Captain America studies him instead of just fighting. He notices Superman's eyes glow red half a beat before the heat vision fires, and that he always checks the civilians behind him before a big move. So Cap fakes openings. His shield is 12 pounds and 2½ feet across, made from an alloy created by accident that nobody has duplicated. It ricochets off a bus, a street light and a building, and each trap is tighter than the last. By the fourth, Superman realizes he's being read, not outmuscled.

The lamppost breaks the pattern. Superman rips one out of the ground and swings it like a bat. The armor can learn a repeating attack, but it can't learn chaos fast enough. Iron Man skids about 24 feet across two lanes of traffic. Cap is peak human, not bulletproof, so Superman pins him to a wall with barely any force. The tier stays at A because nothing they did to his body lasted. The damage was to his confidence.

The Gods

Superman, narrowly, on a thunderclapSuperman vs Thor and Hulk

Thor's lightning hurts for a reason beyond heat. A natural bolt is hotter than the surface of the sun, but the real problem is that Mjolnir is enchanted. Thor's power is Asgardian magic, and Superman has no special resistance to magic, so it goes straight past his invulnerability. Superman catches the hammer mid-swing with one hand and feels his arm strain for the first time all day. Thor then drives a bolt into his face.

Hulk adds the second problem. His rule is that the madder he gets, the harder he hits. That's how he held up a 150 billion ton mountain in 1984's Secret Wars. By the tenth punch, Superman is bleeding from the lip. The comics still lean Superman's way one-on-one. In 1996's DC vs Marvel, readers voted Superman over Hulk. In 2003's JLA/Avengers #3, he stopped Mjolnir one-handed and beat Thor soundly. Editor Tom Brevoort has called letting that happen his greatest crime in comics.

The two-on-one is what makes it close, and speed decides it. Superman won't kill, so every swing has to land hard enough to matter and soft enough to spare them. Hammer from above and fists from the side catch him like a nail between two hammers, and he drops to one knee in the East River. He rips a half-sunken freight truck out of the water and holds it up as a shield. It crumples, and it buys exactly one second.

He uses that second for a thunderclap. Hulk's roar was a pressure spike big enough to blow out every window on the block. Superman's clap is far more. The pressure wave tears across the river and both gods stagger with their ears ringing. Superman moves before either recovers, slams Hulk into the mud and pins Thor's hammer arm with his knee. It won't hold, so he's on top for now. He's also hurt and burning through his stored solar energy.

The Cosmic Tear

D

GalactusSuperman vs Galactus and the Silver Surfer

The Silver Surfer opens with a blast of the Power Cosmic that sends Superman through two office towers. His heat vision bounces off the Surfer's cosmic shell like rain off a windshield. Superman takes the fight into space to protect Manhattan, and there, with no atmosphere filtering it, he gets far more sunlight than at street level, the best charging conditions he'll ever have. For about 10 seconds he matches the Surfer. Then the math turns. This is his fourth fight of the day, and his reserves refill slower than the Surfer's hits drain them.

The Surfer stops his finishing blast because he saw Superman twisting in midair to keep his blows off the city. That's his history. In 1966 Alicia Masters convinced him humanity was worth saving, and he turned on Galactus. He tells Superman there are countless Earths and that Galactus can open the way home if he doesn't eat him first. Superman doesn't weigh the odds. Earth comes first, and he hits Galactus's world-devouring engine head-on.

Galactus doesn't even look. One flick of a finger sends Superman about 239,000 miles to the moon, hard enough to leave a crater miles wide. That's the planet-eater the Surfer once served. In 1999's Superman/Fantastic Four, Galactus first drafted Superman as a herald. Even after Superman broke free, he needed the Fantastic Four's help to fight back.

Fuel decides it. Superman recharges slowly off one star, while Galactus refuels by eating entire worlds. Galactus finally turns and hits him point-blank, and that's Galactus annoyed, not trying. Superman spends every solar reserve just holding together, and his skin goes the gray of old paper. He has maybe five more seconds. The Surfer drags him out of the blast radius by the cape just before the next wave turns the crater to glass. We score that as tier D. He's alive because someone else pulled him out.

What it comes down to

Superman beats every street-level and Earth-bound hero by out-thinking them or holding back, and the comics back him in the Thor and Hulk matchups. It stops at Galactus because of fuel. He recharges slowly from one star, while Galactus eats whole worlds. He survives at tier D only because the Silver Surfer pulls him out, and the one being who could send him home is the one who nearly killed him.
