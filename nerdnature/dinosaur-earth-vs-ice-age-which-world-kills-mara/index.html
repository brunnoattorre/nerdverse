Dinosaur Earth vs Ice Age: Which World Kills Mara?

Wildlife biologist Mara Chen drops onto four lost supercontinents with a knife, a lighter and one quart of water, and we score each world's survivability.

The frozen Ediacaran coast is deadliest because it gives her nothing back.

Every NerdNature fight

Pangaea, the Triassic interior

D

Mara beats the predator, but the heat wins the worldMara Chen vs a Postosuchus-style hunter (and 113°F heat)

The fight turns on a trap she scouted that morning. At a narrow bend, a flood had jammed a dead tree from wall to wall. When the Postosuchus-style hunter lunges, she drops into the channel, scrambles under the trunk and stuffs dry horsetail stems into its branches. The lighter fails once because of sweat on the wheel. The second strike catches, and the flood-dried tree goes up like paper.

With rock on both sides and fire in front, the predator has no way through. A burned predator can't hunt, and one that can't hunt starves, so it backs off. Fire is nothing new here: rocks from this time and place are full of charcoal from wildfires that swept the dry years. She never fights it. She picks the ground and lets the fire do the work.

The predator is dangerous but it isn't the real enemy. It stands 16 to 20 feet long, with a 22-inch skull and serrated teeth nearly three inches long. Its forelimbs were much shorter than its hind limbs, which suggests it ran mostly on two legs. It ambushes rather than chases, and no fossil evidence shows packs. Only about 15% of the vertebrates at a famous New Mexico site from this era are dinosaurs. Crocodile relatives ran the place.

The heat is what drags the world down to a D. Summer afternoons in parts of the interior pass 113°F, and rivers only run in wet years. A person walking in that heat can sweat out about a quart an hour, which is her whole bottle. The horsetails she finds, if they're like modern ones, carry silica and an enzyme that destroys vitamin B1. She spits most of them out. She'd last a few days, maybe less.

Gondwana, the Cretaceous rainforest

C

Mara survives by avoiding the giants and burning off the small huntersMara Chen vs Spinosaurus, Carcharodontosaurus and small night hunters

Water stops being her problem here, and predators take its place. She reads the mud: wide, flat, four-toed prints and a long furrow where something heavy slides in and out of the current. A Spinosaurus-style hunter, up to 46 feet and around 8 tons, works the bank. Whether it swam or ambushed like a giant heron, the water's edge is its kitchen. She holds still, waits for it to drift downstream, then crosses fast and low.

Food nearly beats her. The bright orange seeds in a cycad crown look like candy. Modern cycad seeds carry cycasin, a toxin that attacks the liver, and one seed can poison a dog. With no hospital and no antidote, she lets them rot instead.

At night, two dog-size hunters circle her fire. They aren't raptors: a 2024 study reassigned the so-called raptor teeth from these rocks to other families, including small long-necked noasaurids. They probe and retreat, waiting for her to blink. Near dawn she does, and one rakes her pack strap. She drives a burning branch into its face, and both flee. It's the same rule as the Triassic: a burned hunter is a starving hunter.

The far bank gives her a spring under a cliff overhang. Her fire then draws something much bigger, since these rocks also hold Carcharodontosaurus, with serrated teeth up to 8 inches long. She climbs the rock behind the spring to a ledge nothing that heavy can reach. Water and shelter earn the C. But a paleontologist who studied these rocks called this arguably the most dangerous place in Earth's history. Three of the largest predatory dinosaurs found, plus crocodile-like hunters, packed in together.

Laurasia, the Jurassic floodplain and forest

B

Mara, who steers the herd into the AllosaurusMara Chen vs an Allosaurus-style predator and a sauropod herd

The Allosaurus-style hunter, about 28 feet and two tons, stalks the fringe of a sauropod herd. Its bite was weak for its size. One model puts it near 3,600 newtons, a tenth or less of a T. rex's. The weapon was the skull, swung down like a hatchet with jaws gaping past 90 degrees, then pulled back to slice. That works as a slow kill on a sauropod. Against a person it needs no time.

Mara can't outrun it in a straight line. The Allosaurus is at least as fast as she is. She exploits turning. A 2001 study argued big meat-eating dinosaurs were badly limited by rotational inertia, with a long tail and a lot of mass resisting every change of direction. So she zigzags, and each cut costs it more than it costs her. She slides under the arch of two sauropod legs, and it won't follow into that forest of legs.

The decisive move is her fire. Pinned between 20-ton legs and a predator waiting where the herd thins, she lights a fist of resin-soaked needles and throws them at the nearest giant's heels. The spooked sauropod lurches toward the edge where the Allosaurus waits, and the line surges with it. A sauropod tail can whip at around 60 mph. One catches the predator across the legs, and a flank shoulders into its ribs. It limps for the trees.

That limp fits the fossils: Big Al, the most famous Allosaurus ever dug up, carries at least 19 injuries. The B comes from what the Jurassic gives her. The creeks hold fish and freshwater clams, and the conifers give cover and fuel. There is no fruit, because flowering plants won't exist for another 20 million years or so. She lives on timing, not strength, and a predator this size forgives nothing.

Pannotia and the Ice Age margins

F

The Ice Age coast; Mara can't survive itMara Chen vs the Gaskiers ice age coast

Nothing here hunts her. The danger is the world itself. About 580 million years ago, in the Gaskiers glaciation, the sun gives light without warmth. Even the air is in doubt. One 2022 reconstruction puts oxygen at 1 to 20% of today's level. The top of that range is about 4% oxygen, which would knock a person out in under a minute. Other methods read much higher, and we give her the generous number. Otherwise the chapter is one sentence long.

Cold does the killing. A resting human is a small furnace, and in this cold it has to burn harder just to hold core temperature. Shivering can push heat output to five times resting, and every minute of it burns fuel. Her tank is already low after three worlds of barely eating. Then she searches the shore for food and finds none. The first land plants are more than 100 million years away, and the first known land animal, a millipede, is about 150 million years away.

What's left is a thin crust of microbes on the rocks and slimy mats in the shallows. A few soft fronds sit on the deep seafloor, out of her reach. Nothing she can reach is food. Her best play is the snow cave she digs in a whiteout. Snow is mostly trapped air, and a well-built cave can sit around 32°F inside while it's 40 below outside. It's the right call, but it can't win.

The lighter, which saved her in every other world, buys nothing here. Nothing on this continent has ever grown a stem, so there is nothing to burn. Every bit of warmth she makes comes out of her own body, and nothing on the coast pays it back. That makes it an F. She has days at most, and every one is downhill.

What it comes down to

Mara never loses a fight to claws. She beats a Postosuchus-style hunter with fire, avoids Spinosaurus and Carcharodontosaurus, and uses a sauropod herd against an Allosaurus. What sets the tiers is whether a world pays her back with water, food and fuel. The Jurassic gives her all three and earns a B. The Triassic heat, with its toxic horsetails, earns a D, and the Cretaceous rainforest gets a C. The Ice Age coast has no predators, but it has nothing to eat or burn, so it is the one world she can't survive.
